What does the volume tell us?

For λ ≠ 0, the arrows fill three dimensions: every initial-data triple can be formed in exactly one way from these arrows. Equivalently, the three independent solutions can match any prescribed y(a), y′(a), and y″(a) with exactly one coefficient triple.

For λ = 0, all arrows lie in y″(a) = 0. Every combination has zero second derivative, so this chosen set cannot represent a solution with y″(a) = 1. The original ODE still has a solution for that initial condition; the chosen set of solutions is incomplete.

These functions all solve the same regular homogeneous linear equation on ℝ. More generally, the Wronskian test for n solutions uses a common normalized homogeneous equation of order n with continuous coefficients on the whole interval. For arbitrary functions, W ≡ 0 alone does not imply dependence.

Why can W be negative when volume cannot?

The Wronskian is a signed volume for the ordered arrows v₁, v₂, v₃. In this example, W(a) = λ. A positive or negative λ puts the third arrow on opposite sides of the base plane. The ordinary geometric volume is |W(a)| = |λ|.

Swapping the order of two arrows also reverses the sign of W while preserving the geometric volume. For two solutions, the corresponding picture is the signed area of a parallelogram.
