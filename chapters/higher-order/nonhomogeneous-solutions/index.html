Why does this describe every solution?

Let Y be any solution of Y″ − 4Y = 12, and let yₚ be one particular solution. Subtract their equations:

L[Y − yₚ] = 12 − 12 = 0.

Thus Y − yₚ is a homogeneous solution. Since e2x and e−2x form a fundamental set, Y − yₚ = c₁e2x + c₂e−2x. Every nonhomogeneous solution is therefore yc + yp.

More generally, for a normalized linear equation of order n with continuous coefficients and forcing on an open interval I, choose a fundamental set y₁, …, yₙ of homogeneous solutions and one particular solution yₚ on I. The general solution is y = c₁y₁ + ⋯ + cₙyₙ + yₚ.

Why is the particular solution not unique?

The difference between −3 + e2x and −3 is the homogeneous solution e2x. Switching to the second choice gives the identity

c₁e2x + c₂e−2x − 3 = (c₁ − 1)e2x + c₂e−2x + (−3 + e2x).

The decomposition changes, while the total solution and its initial conditions stay fixed. With unrestricted coefficients, both choices describe exactly the same family.

A sum of two nonhomogeneous solutions generally has L[Y₁ + Y₂] = 24. Their difference has L[Y₁ − Y₂] = 0 and solves the associated homogeneous equation.
